FateDriver: A Dual-View Graph Neural Network for Predicting Driver Genes in Transcription Factor-Induced Cellular Reprogramming

Reprogramming protocols deliver a handful of transcription factors, but the endogenous regulators working alongside them are only partly known. FateDriver takes the delivered factors as its positive labels, learns how they align with the source→target direction, and returns the unlabeled genes that align the same way.

Jieun Sung*, Kyuwon Son*, Hyuk-Jin Cha, Wankyu Kim, Haeseung Lee · *equal contribution · in preparation

Problem

Cellular reprogramming converts one lineage into another through delivered factors — OSKM for pluripotency, GMT for cardiac conversion. But the delivered factors are not the whole story: the resulting fate is governed by intrinsic regulatory networks, and the endogenous genes that orchestrate the conversion are largely unknown.

Existing methods infer those regulators from network structure, from expression dynamics along a trajectory, or from both — CEFCON applies network control theory to a learned GRN, SwitchTFI fits a decision-tree switch to each edge, TENET scores transfer entropy along pseudotime, Fatecode perturbs an autoencoder latent code. All four share one blind spot: none of them learns from the factors already known to drive the system.

They also supervise on regulatory edges or on cell labels rather than on genes, so a gene is rankable only if some prior network or transcription-factor annotation admits it. Each ranking therefore covers only part of the measured transcriptome.

Approach

The premise is simple: the delivered factors are known drivers, so a gene that aligns with the transition the same way they do is a candidate for a shared role. Making that computable requires cells and genes to live in one space.

  • Cell encoder (view 1). Cells are joined to nearest neighbours in expression space, but an edge is kept only if it advances in pseudotime, running from the earlier cell to the later one. A graph convolutional network embeds cells over that directed graph, so the geometry carries transcriptional similarity and the direction of the transition.
  • Gene encoder (view 2). Each gene is placed in the same space by combining a learnable per-gene embedding with the embeddings of the cells expressing it, weighted by expression, then refined over a prior gene network (the NicheNet regulatory and signaling union) so positions reflect known regulation rather than expression alone.
  • Gene scorer. A set encoder summarises the source and target populations into order-invariant vectors. Each gene is compared against source, target and their difference in two complementary ways — a similarity term collapsing the three into one direction, and a cross-attention term that keeps them apart and asks which the gene belongs to. A transition prior adds evidence read straight from the data.

The labels are the hard part. Only three or four genes per system are known drivers. The unlabeled remainder is not a set of confirmed non-drivers — those genes have simply never been tested. Treating them as negatives would penalise exactly the genes of interest, so the objective is a positive–unlabeled ranking problem over three tiers: delivered factors, their direct downstream targets, then the remainder. Placing targets below the factors rather than alongside them separates genes that drive a transition from genes that merely respond to it. A gene that repeatedly scores at the top is moved into a pseudo-positive bank rather than being punished for it.

Three losses combine, weighted to take effect in turn: a reconstruction loss anchors the shared space to the measured counts, a ranking loss supplies the tiered supervision, and a shift-consistency loss keeps the top of the ranking aligned with the direction of the transition.

Results

Benchmarked on three scRNA-seq datasets spanning both conversion systems — two OSKM-driven human iPSC datasets (GSE297234, GSE242423) and one GMT-driven mouse cardiac dataset (GSE98567) — against the four existing methods plus an expression-only MLP sharing FateDriver’s labels, splits and objective.

Does it recover independently curated regulators? Scored as AUROC against externally curated reprogramming enhancers and barriers, where 0.5 is chance. FateDriver was highest on two datasets (0.93 and 0.74) and second on the third (0.87). TENET reached 0.90 on one, but ranked only 387 genes and covered three curated positives against ten for FateDriver; its cardiac AUROC was 0.41.

Is it just finding co-expressed genes? This is the result that separates the two. The MLP produced the top-100 genes most correlated with the delivered factors in every dataset (r = 0.87, 0.96, 0.60) yet recovered less of the curated set than FateDriver, whose own co-expression was low (0.17, 0.15, 0.47). Sharing an expression pattern with the delivered factors and recovering curated regulators do not track each other — and FateDriver is doing the second, not the first.

A score belongs to a transition, not to a gene. Two transcriptionally distinct fibroblast states from one heterogeneous culture were scored against a common iPSC target. The rankings differed over four-fifths of their content: 82 genes specific to each state, only 18 shared. Those 18 were the four delivered factors plus the senescence barrier Cdkn1a, Cdkn2a and Serpine1. The two sets also moved in opposite directions along the trajectory — 79 of 82 baseline-specific genes switched on, 75 of 79 primed-specific genes switched off — and each kept its direction when scored along a fate axis neither ranking had used.

Partial versus complete reprogramming. Scored against two endpoints reached from the same fibroblasts, FateDriver returned different gene sets. Among the genes specific to the partial endpoint, DDX58 (RIG-I, which initiates the type I interferon response) best separated single cells that activated the endogenous pluripotency program from those that did not (AUROC 0.711 young donor, 0.851 aged). The relationship is graded rather than all-or-none: across eight strata of interferon-stimulated gene score, the fraction of day-10 cells that reprogrammed fell from 32.8% to 1.2% in the young donor and 26.1% to 0.4% in the aged.

Transfer to a dataset with no defined source. Applied to a population partially reprogrammed in vivo in mouse intestine, FateDriver ranked crypt base columnar cells highest among candidate origins (source affinity 0.396, 1.7× the next candidate). CytoTRACE2, an independent measure, scored CBC highest for developmental plasticity — support from a method that shares none of FateDriver’s assumptions.
