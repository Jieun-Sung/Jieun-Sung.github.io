Sequence-Similarity–Guided Multi-Representation Ensemble for MAP3K5 IC50 Prediction

Predicting MAP3K5 (ASK1) inhibitor potency from molecular structure, where the target-specific labelled data is far too small to train on. The approach borrows labelled compounds from homologous kinases and weights how much to trust them by sequence similarity.

3rd AI Drug Discovery Competition · Korea Pharmaceutical and Bio-Pharma Association · third place of 1,137 participants

  • 3rd of 1,137 participants
  • Uncertainty-based ensemble learning
  • Poster

Problem

The task was to predict IC50 for MAP3K5 (ASK1) inhibitors from molecular structure alone. The difficulty is not the model class but the data: measurements against MAP3K5 itself are far too few to train a competitive regressor on.

Data for other kinases is abundant. The question is whether it can be borrowed without corrupting the prediction — and how much to trust a measurement made against a different protein.

Approach

Borrow by homology, weight by similarity. IC50 data was collected across many proteins from PubChem, BindingDB, CAS and ChEMBL — 381,152 measurements between 241,586 compounds and 389 proteins. Kinases have highly conserved active sites, so proteins similar to MAP3K5 were selected by PSI-BLAST. The BLAST score was converted to a z-score and passed through a sigmoid, giving a normalised similarity zB used both as a model input and as an ensemble weight.

Three representations, because none is obviously right. Each was pretrained differently and each sees a molecule differently, so each was given its own model predicting both pIC50 and its own uncertainty:

  • KPGT — molecule as a hypergraph, pretrained with a Graph Transformer.
  • ChemBERTa — SMILES tokenized into a RoBERTa model, pretrained by masked-input property prediction.
  • SELFIES-TED — SELFIES, a string representation designed so every string is a valid molecule, with a BART model pretrained on 80M ZINC compounds.

Combining them. Each model’s weight comes from its predicted uncertainty, then that weight is blended toward a uniform 1/3 according to how similar the assay target is to MAP3K5: wiB = zB · wi + (1 − zB) · 1/3. When the data comes from a close homologue the model-specific weighting is trusted; when it comes from a distant protein the ensemble falls back toward equal weighting rather than committing to a confident but poorly grounded prediction.

The loss has two parts: a confidence-weighted MSE, in which the BLAST score scales each example’s contribution, and an uncertainty loss based on negative log-likelihood that calibrates predicted uncertainty against actual error and suppresses overconfidence. The two are combined linearly with α = 0.1.

Results

Contribution of each representation was assessed on a fixed test set. Individually the ranking was KPGT > SELFIES-TED > ChemBERTa, but the combination of all three scored highest on the competition validation set (private score 0.648). Weighting the ensemble by BLAST target similarity outperformed a model that instead learned protein sequence embeddings through cross-attention.

Third place out of 1,137 participants. The approach is not specific to MAP3K5 — it applies to any kinase with sparse labels, and extends beyond IC50 to drug–target interaction regression more generally.

This was competition work rather than a paper, so the poster is the primary record. It is included here because the data-scarcity problem it addresses — borrowing supervision from related targets and calibrating how much to trust it — recurs throughout the rest of this research.
